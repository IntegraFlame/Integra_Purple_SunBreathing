The corpus callosum is a large white matter tract that connects the two hemispheres of the brain. This bundle of nerve fibers, facilitates the interhemispheric communication between the left and right cerebral hemispheres and it plays a critical role in integrating motor, sensory and cognitive functions of the brain. 

The name "corpus callosum" originates from Latin, meaning "tough body." It is the largest white matter structure in the brain both in terms of size (around 700 square millimeters for the midsagittal cross-section) and number of axonal projections (around 200 million axons) between the two hemispheres.

Functions & Roles  
   
Visual Integration: Combines the left and right visual fields to create depth perception (stereoscopic vision). Without it, you would see two independent visual worlds. \[1\]\[8\]  
Bimanual Coordination: Enables complex movements requiring both hands, such as tying shoelaces, typing, or playing the piano. It suppresses "mirror movements" so one hand can do something different from the other. \[9\]\[23\]  
Attention Monitoring: Allows the Right Hemisphere (the "Monitor") to interrupt the Left Hemisphere (the "Doer") when an error is detected or a distraction appears. \[21\]

A congenital condition where the bridge never develops. People with ACC often have difficulty understanding social cues, lack abstract problem-solving skills, and may have a high pain tolerance. Prefrontal Cortex  
Located immediately behind your forehead, the Prefrontal Cortex (PFC) is the brain's newest and most advanced evolution. It serves as the "CEO of the Brain," responsible for high-level management: planning complex cognitive behavior, personality expression, decision making, and moderating social behavior.  
While the rest of the brain handles sensory input (what you see) and motor output (movement), the PFC handles "Top-Down Processing": it overrides your primitive impulses to ensure your actions align with your long-term goals.   
domain icon  
The Three Departments  
   
Neuroscientists divide the PFC into specialized sub-regions, each managing a different aspect of your life.   
Dorsolateral PFC (The Architect): Handles "Cold Cognition": pure logic, working memory, and planning. It holds the rules of a task in your head and figures out the steps needed to solve a puzzle.  
Orbitofrontal Cortex (The Diplomat): Handles "Hot Cognition": emotion and social regulation. It connects directly to the emotional centers (amygdala) to tell you, "Do not say that offensive joke right now."  
Ventromedial PFC (The Judge): Processes Risk and Reward. It gives you that "gut feeling" about whether a decision is good or bad based on past emotional experiences.

Interactive: The Planning Challenge  
One of the primary functions of the Dorsolateral PFC is sequencing: the ability to break a complex goal into ordered steps. This is often tested using the Tower of Hanoi, a puzzle that forces you to think several moves ahead and temporarily move pieces away from the goal to solve the problem. 

The PFC is the last part of the brain to mature. While the sensory and emotional areas are fully active in teenagers, the "brake pedal" (PFC) is still under construction. This gap explains why adolescents often engage in risky behavior despite knowing the dangers—their engine is ready, but their steering system is not.   
Synaptic Pruning: The brain eliminates unused connections to make the remaining circuits more efficient.  
Myelination: The nerve fibers are coated in white fatty tissue (white matter), increasing processing speed by up to 100x.

The most famous case study in neuroscience, Phineas Gage, revealed the specific function of the PFC. In 1848, an iron rod was driven through his frontal lobe. He survived and retained his intelligence, memory, and speech, but his personality was destroyed.   
"No Longer Gage"  
Before the accident, he was a responsible foreman. Afterward, he became impulsive, profane, and unable to stick to plans. This proved that the PFC is not necessary for living, but it is essential for being who you are.
